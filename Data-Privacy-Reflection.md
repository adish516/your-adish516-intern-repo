# Data Privacy Reflection

## What I learned from Focus Bear’s privacy policy

One thing that stood out to me is that even a habit someone adds could
reveal something personal, like their health or religion. So information
doesn’t have to look sensitive at first to deserve careful handling.

The policy says Focus Bear collects data for specific purposes and
uses external providers to help deliver its services. It also explains
that users can request access to, correction of or deletion of their
data, subject to the relevant conditions.

My main takeaway is that being able to access information doesn’t mean
I can use it for whatever I want and be mindful.

## What information I would treat as confidential

I would treat user details, private feedback, account information and
anything about someone’s health as confidential.

For my partnership role, I would also be careful with private partner
conversations, contact lists, agreements and company plans that haven’t
been made public. If I’m unsure whether something can be shared, I’d
rather check than assume it’s fine.

## How I would handle data in my daily work

I would only access the information I need for the task and keep it
in the tools approved by the team. I wouldn’t make extra copies in
personal accounts just because it’s more convenient.

Before sending a message or sharing a document, I would check who
it’s going to and whether they need everything included.

I’d also check screenshots before uploading them, especially because
my onboarding repository is public. Something in the background could
include an email address, private message or access token.

## Storing, sharing and deleting information

I would keep work documents in the agreed company location and give
access only to the people who need it. I’d avoid setting a document
to “anyone with the link” unless that’s actually intended and approved.

I wouldn’t paste private company or user information into an AI tool
without checking that the tool and that use are allowed.

When information is no longer needed, I would follow the company’s
retention and deletion process. I wouldn’t delete shared records on
my own, but I’d check how to remove unnecessary downloads or copies
safely.

## Common mistakes to avoid

I think privacy mistakes can happen through small things, like choosing
the wrong email recipient, attaching the wrong file or forgetting that
a document link is public.

Another mistake would be assuming that removing someone’s name makes
the information anonymous. Other details could still identify them.

Taking a moment to check the recipient, attachment and sharing settings
before sending would help prevent these mistakes.

## What I would do if something went wrong

If I accidentally shared something or noticed a possible breach, I’d
report it straight away to my supervisor or the designated privacy
contact. I wouldn’t wait until I was completely sure or try to hide it.

I would explain what was shared, when it happened and who might have
access. If possible, I would stop further sharing or restrict the link,
while keeping a record of what happened.

I would then follow the team’s instructions rather than try to handle
everything myself or contact affected users without guidance.

## One habit I will follow

Before sending or uploading anything, I’ll do a quick check of like: Does it contain private information?

It’s a small step, but it’s something I can build into how I work.
